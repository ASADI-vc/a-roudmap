# src-stanford-canaries2025
- **Title:** Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence
- **Author / Organization:** Erik Brynjolfsson (Stanford University & NBER), Bharat Chandar (Stanford University), Ruyu Chen (Stanford University); Stanford Digital Economy Lab (working paper)
- **Canonical URL:** https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publications/canaries-in-the-coal-mine/ (revised paper PDF: https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/app/uploads/2026/08/Canaries_August2026.pdf)
- **Publication / update date:** Originally published August 2025; landing page shows "Revised August 12 2026" version using ADP data through June 2026. This note records both the original 2025 headline and the revised figures.
- **Access date:** 2026-11-14
- **Source type:** academic preprint
- **Research questions addressed:** RQ6 (employment/junior demand changes) — direct evidence on entry-level employment in AI-exposed occupations, incl. software development; minimal RQ7 content
- **Original summary (own words):** Using high-frequency ADP payroll records for millions of US workers, the authors find no evidence of economy-wide AI job displacement, but a persistent, widening gap for the youngest workers: employment of 22–25-year-olds in AI-exposed occupations sits 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with less-exposed peers (June 2026), while experienced workers show no comparable gap. The change runs through reduced hiring, not layoffs, is concentrated where AI substitutes for tasks (flat or rising where AI complements), and shows up in employment rather than pay. The authors explicitly frame this as descriptive early-warning evidence, not causal proof.
- **Relevant findings:**
  - **Headline fact (revised, Aug 2026):** "employment of young workers (ages 22–25) in AI-exposed occupations now stands 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with that of their less-exposed peers" (US, ADP data through June 2026).
  - **Original 2025 headline:** "Earlier versions of this study headlined regression estimates adjusting for firm-level shocks (a 13% relative decline as of July 2025 data; 16% as of September 2025 data). We now emphasize the simpler descriptive divergence... the kept-pace shortfall was 15% at the July 2025 data vintage and has since widened to 19% as of June 2026."
  - **Levels (US, Nov 2022 – June 2026):** "employment of 22–25-year-olds in the two most exposed quintiles fell about 11%... while employment of the same age group in the three least-exposed quintiles grew about 10%" — "a divergence of 21 percentage points, or 19% relative to growth for the bottom three quintiles."
  - **Six facts:** (1) no evidence of widespread, economy-wide job displacement; (2) the 19% young-worker gap with no comparable gap for experienced workers; (3) divergence widened steadily since first documented August 2025; (4) "operates primarily through reduced hiring of young workers rather than increased separations"; (5) declines concentrated in occupations where AI usage substitutes for human tasks; complementary usage → flat or rising employment, especially for experienced workers; (6) "Adjustment is occurring through employment rather than base compensation."
  - **Occupation case studies:** employment decline in AI-exposed jobs is illustrated with case studies "such as software development and customer service" (Appendix Fig. B.3, H.1); per-occupation single-number declines for software developers are not stated in text. In CPS microdata the authors note only "between 23 and 48 young software developers aged between 22 and 25 per month" are observed, making such subgroup estimates highly volatile.
  - **Regional variation (most exposed quintile of young workers, from Nov 2022 level):** West about −21%, Northeast −13%, South −7%, Midwest −9% (US, 2026 revision).
  - **Alternative exposure measures:** with other AI-exposure indices, "for workers aged 22–25, employment falls in the most exposed quintile, by 10 to 13% relative to November 2022" (US).
  - **Compensation nuance:** "Perhaps one slight exception to the muted compensation patterns is software developers, for whom compensation for 22–25 year-olds grew somewhat faster than for older workers in the period after ChatGPT," possibly composition effects (e.g., firms contracting hiring of lower-paid engineers).
  - **Robustness/persistence:** divergence "persists when excluding technology firms and computer occupations, when controlling for exposure to interest-rate increases and for remote work, and across alternative measures of AI exposure."
  - **Iran note:** No Iran-specific data — the study is US-only (ADP US payroll records).
- **Population / methodology / tools:** Anonymized ADP administrative payroll data — "ADP provides payroll services to firms employing over 26 million U.S. workers"; analysis sample is "a balanced panel of firms observed monthly from January 2021 through June 2026, comprising between 3.5 and 5 million employees per month" (extended balanced sample from January 2018 for pre-ChatGPT trends); full-time workers under age 70 with positive earnings. Workers linked to AI exposure via ADP's internal taxonomy mapping job titles to 2010 SOC codes (job titles missing for ~30%, imputed from workers' adjacent codes); exposure measures incl. Eloundou et al. (2024) GPT-4 scores and others; comparisons with CPS and ACS benchmarks. Public companion: AI Economic Indicators (digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/project/indicators/).
- **Limitations / conflicts of interest / applicability:** Authors' own caveats: "We interpret these facts as early, descriptive indicators—canaries in the coal mine—rather than causal estimates." (1) Pre-existing divergent trends predating ChatGPT, particularly around COVID-19; (2) estimates attenuate when controlling for occupational education levels (either an alternative explanation or the channel AI operates through); (3) divergence is "more pronounced in the ADP analysis sample than in national survey benchmarks" — ADP sample overrepresents manufacturing/wholesale, larger firms and higher-AI-exposure occupations, and underrepresents retail/accommodation-food; ~30% missing job titles imputed; entry/exit of new firms not captured in the balanced panel. Interpretation was publicly contested (interest-rate/sector confounds); the authors addressed critiques in a February 9, 2026 update ("Canaries, Interest Rates, and Timing...") and an October 10, 2025 essay ("AI and Labor Markets: What We Know and Don't Know"). US-only, full-time workers, so not generalizable globally or to informal labor markets; short post-ChatGPT window ≠ long-run equilibrium.
- **Candidate claims supported:**
  - observed: entry-level (22–25) employment in AI-exposed US occupations ~11% below Nov 2022 levels by June 2026 while least-exposed quintiles grew ~10% (ADP, 3.5–5M employees/month).
  - observed (survey-adjacent descriptive): young-worker shortfall of 19% relative to kept-pace counterfactual as of June 2026; 13% headline in the original Aug 2025 version (July 2025 data vintage).
  - causal (explicitly disclaimed as descriptive): declines attributed primarily to AI substituting for tasks in exposed occupations — the paper reports robustness to confound controls but calls itself "rather than causal estimates."
  - observed: adjustment via reduced hiring, not separations, and via employment, not base pay (US, Nov 2022–June 2026).
- **Implications for learners:** The entry-level squeeze is a hiring-threshold problem, not mass displacement: junior openings in AI-exposed fields (incl. software development) shrink first, so new learners face a higher bar to a first role; roles where AI complements the worker remain flat or growing, and experienced workers show no gap. Signals: build skills in AI-complementary work, target less AI-substitutable or tacit-knowledge tasks, and expect first-job entry to be the hardest step — mid-career mobility appears less affected.
