# src-metr2025
- **Title:** Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity
- **Author / Organization:** Joel Becker, Nate Rush, Beth Barnes, David Rein — METR (nonprofit AI research org, donation-funded)
- **Canonical URL:** https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/ (the URL in the task list, `metr.org/research/research-2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-productivity/`, returns 404; canonical found via METR's research index; an arXiv version is linked from the page)
- **Publication / update date:** 2025-07-10. Page carries an update banner (seen 2026-11-14): "In February 2026, we published new data on the productivity impact of late-2025 AI tools" and warns these historical results "no longer reflect the current impact of AI models" in their view.
- **Access date:** 2026-11-14
- **Source type:** controlled experiment (randomized controlled trial, published as blog post + arXiv paper by a nonprofit)
- **Research questions addressed:** RQ4 (does AI assistance improve productivity, under what conditions); the belief-vs-reality gap also bears on RQ7 (practitioner perception/trust)
- **Original summary (own words):** METR ran an RCT with 16 experienced open-source developers working on 246 real issues in large repositories they knew well; each issue was randomly assigned to allow or disallow AI use. Allowed tools were primarily Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet. Surprisingly, allowing AI made them 19% slower, while the same developers had forecast a 24% speedup beforehand and, even after experiencing the slowdown, estimated a 20% speedup afterward. METR examined 20 potential explanatory factors and found evidence that 5 likely contribute. The authors frame the result as a snapshot of early-2025 tools in one specific setting and explicitly list over-generalizations they do not endorse.
- **Relevant findings:**
  - Developers "take 19% longer to complete issues" when allowed to use AI tools (headline slowdown).
  - Belief gap: developers expected AI to speed them up by 24% before the study; after the study they still believed AI had sped them up by 20%.
  - 16 experienced developers recruited from large open-source repositories "averaging 22k+ stars and 1M+ lines of code" contributed to for multiple years; 246 issues total (bug fixes, features, refactors); tasks average two hours each.
  - "We investigate 20 potential factors that might explain the slowdown, finding evidence that 5 likely contribute."
  - Ruled-out artifacts: developers "submitted similar quality PRs with and without AI," complied with assignment, did not differentially drop issues; slowdown persists across outcome measures, estimator methodologies, and subsets.
  - Tool context: chat, Cursor agent mode, autocomplete; Cursor "may not use optimal prompting/scaffolding" and does not sample many tokens.
- **Population / methodology / tools:** n=16 experienced OSS maintainers; 246 issues randomized within-developer to AI-allowed vs AI-disallowed; screen-recorded sessions; self-reported implementation time; $150/hr compensation; clustered standard errors across 16 developers; alternative estimators (incl. naive ratio estimator) give similar results. Tools: any chosen, primarily Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet ("frontier models at the time").
- **Limitations / conflicts of interest / applicability:** Small sample (16 devs, 246 issues) — authors say it has "just enough" statistical power to reject zero effect. Experienced developers on familiar, mature, high-quality repos; task-level (individual issues), not end-to-end team delivery. Possible participation bias (developers convinced of large speedups may have declined; authors "can't rule this out"). Learning effects beyond ~50 hours of Cursor usage can't be ruled out. Self-reported time (with screen recordings as context). Early-2025 tools; METR itself now flags these results as historical (Feb 2026 follow-up exists). Not vendor-funded, but METR's mission (AI capability/AI-R&D acceleration measurement) shapes framing; authors state they "initially were broadly expecting to see positive speedup."
- **Candidate claims supported:**
  - **causal** (RCT): for this population/tasks, early-2025 AI tooling caused a ~19% slowdown.
  - **survey/observed:** developers systematically misjudge AI's effect on their speed (+24% forecast, +20% retrospective estimate vs −19% actual).
  - **observed:** slowdown is robust across estimators; PR quality similar in both arms.
  - **recommendation:** use diverse evaluation methodologies; self-reports of speedup "can be very inaccurate."
- **Implications for learners:** Productivity effects are task- and context-dependent — METR says it is "plausible or likely that AI tools are useful in many other contexts," e.g., less experienced developers or unfamiliar codebases. Learners should measure their own throughput rather than trust intuition, and treat perceived speedups skeptically. Also relevant: perceived non-productivity benefits (enjoyment, skill investment) can coexist with measured slowdowns.
